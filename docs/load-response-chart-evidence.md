# Load response chart: evidence and display rules

Reviewed 28 September 2026 for the Classic client Progress workspace.

- The [athlete training-load monitoring consensus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28463642/) supports examining multiple individual training and response measures over time. The chart therefore offers dated metrics the app records for the selected client: session RPE load and ingredients, resistance and conditioning observations, wellness check-ins, wearable readings, and the existing ACWR, monotony and strain calculations. Unrecorded metrics show an empty state. It does not generate a new response score or prescribe training.
- The [Australian Institute of Sport HRV guidance](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/position_statements/heart-rate-variability) treats HRV and resting heart rate as contextual observations affected by how they are measured. The chart keeps their units and source labels visible; a load–HRV pattern is not interpreted as a causal response or clearance decision.
- The 7- and 28-day rolling options are descriptive arithmetic means over **recorded values in that calendar window**. They retain gaps on unobserved dates, report the count of observed days with every table value, and do not impute missing days as rest. The raw view remains available. Rolling options change only the display, not any underlying load calculation.
- X-axis date shows time series. Selecting a metric for X pairs X and Y values **only on the same date**, as a scatter plot. Line or column encodings are limited to the date view because connecting or binning a metric X-axis would add an order or aggregation the records do not supply. The dated table is the exact-value and source alternative to pointer inspection.
- An optional second Y-axis meets the trainer’s comparison need, with explicit units, distinct series colours, and a warning against reading crossings or matching slopes as a relationship. The [UK Office for National Statistics’ chart guidance](https://digitalblog.ons.gov.uk/2019/07/03/dueling-with-axis-the-problems-with-dual-axis-charts/) explains why independently scaled dual axes can mislead. It is off by default.

Multiple entries on one date are summed for additive exposure measures and averaged for ratings/readings. No universal thresholds, correlation estimates, injury predictions, or causal claims are shown. Derived ACWR, monotony, and strain retain the existing formulas and their existing availability rules.
